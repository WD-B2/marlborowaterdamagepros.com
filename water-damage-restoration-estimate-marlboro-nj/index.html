What a Water Damage Estimate Should Include: Marlboro, NJ

A good estimate reads like a work order. A bad one reads like a number with a signature line under it.

By the Marlboro Water Damage Pros team

Call (732) 352-9660

A written water damage estimate should list each room, the materials affected, the equipment going in, the expected drying days, what gets torn out and hauled away, and what is left out for the rebuild. If those pieces are missing, you are looking at a total with no way to check it against the work.

This guide walks through each line a Marlboro homeowner should expect, the red flags that show up in thin estimates, and a simple method for comparing two of them. It gives no prices, because price depends on the job. It shows you what a price should be attached to.

Estimate, scope and invoice are three different papers

An estimate is the company's expected cost for the work as described. A scope of work is the description itself: which rooms, which materials, which tasks, in what order. An invoice is what you are billed after the work is done, and it should match the scope.

Many problems start when the first paper is a number without the second. A total of one figure for the whole house tells you nothing about what is being done in the kitchen versus the basement. Ask for the scope in writing and ask that the estimate refer to it line by line.

Also ask what changes the number. Water jobs shift when demolition reveals more wet material than expected, so a sound estimate says how added findings are handled and who approves them before work continues.

Rooms, measurements and moisture readings

A solid estimate starts from measurements: the affected rooms, the approximate square footage of wet floor and the height wet wall materials rose. It should also record moisture readings from a meter, and thermal imaging where the crew used it to trace wet areas behind walls.

Square footage appears in the document as a measurement of the job, not as a price multiplier. Be cautious of any figure presented as a flat amount per square foot with nothing else attached, because water jobs differ by material, water category and drying difficulty.

If you see a moisture map or a list of readings by room, keep it. That record shows where drying started and gives the crew and your insurer a baseline to compare with the finished readings.

Equipment and drying days

Equipment is where estimates differ most. Look for named categories: extraction units for standing water, air movers for airflow, LGR dehumidifiers for pulling moisture from the air, and specialty drying for floors or wall cavities when needed.

The estimate should also state the expected drying days and how the crew will verify them. Drying usually runs several days for a typical room, but the right number comes from daily meter readings, not from a fixed schedule. A good estimate says the days are expected and subject to readings.

If you are reading a quote for structural drying, ask how the crew decides when to remove equipment and what readings it needs to see. The answer should reference a dry standard, not a feeling.

Demolition, disposal and what gets saved

Demolition is the cutting out of materials that cannot be dried or are contaminated. The estimate should list which materials come out, such as baseboards, the lower section of drywall, wet insulation, carpet pad or damaged flooring, and say whether disposal is included.

Ceilings need their own look. A sagging or stained ceiling may come down, or a section may be cut back to dry framing. If you are comparing quotes that include ceiling water damage repair, check that each one names what is removed above the ceiling as well as the finished surface.

Ask the company to point to what is being saved. Drying in place, when materials allow, can cost less and reduce rebuild work. A line that says everything gets removed deserves a question about why.

Rebuild exclusions and the gap between drying and finished

Many restoration estimates end at dry and cleaned. New drywall, paint, flooring, trim and cabinets may be listed as excluded, or priced in a second document. That is not a trick, but you should see it before you sign.

Ask for a plain statement of where the estimate stops. If the rebuild is a separate scope, ask whether the same company can do it and whether you will receive a second written estimate.

After flood losses, the gap can be larger. Water from outside is Category 2 or 3, so more materials are removed and the rebuild list grows. A crew handling flood damage restoration can explain what the demolition list looks like once the water is out.

Red flags in a thin estimate

Some warning signs show up again and again. None proves a company is dishonest, but each is a reason to ask more questions.

A single lump total with no rooms, materials or equipment listed. A demand to sign before anyone has measured or metered anything. Pressure to decide immediately, or a price that changes on the phone without a change to the scope. No mention of water category, no mention of drying monitoring, and no mention of what is excluded.

  • Verbal promises that are not in the written scope.
  • A claim that your insurer will cover everything, since only the insurer decides coverage.
  • A request for full payment before work starts.
  • No contractor registration information offered when you ask for it.

Comparing two estimates fairly

Put the two documents side by side and compare scope before price. If one lists a dehumidifier for six days and the other lists none, the totals describe different jobs.

Work down the same checklist for each: rooms, materials, equipment, drying days, demolition, disposal, exclusions. Mark every difference. Then call both companies and ask them to explain the gaps. The lower total is only lower if it covers the same work.

Do not choose on speed alone either. Same-day response when available matters on an active leak, but the estimate you sign should still match what the crew will do on site.

What Marlboro homeowners should know

The Marlboro Township Construction Division permit page says building and remodeling generally require a permit, and that applications include drawings and an estimate or materials list. It also says home improvement contractors must be registered with the New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs and show their registration card before permits are issued. The page does not mention water damage repairs specifically, so ask the Construction Division whether your rebuild needs a permit.

That makes registration a practical question for your estimate. Ask the contractor for its registration details before you sign, especially for rebuild work. A crew serving Marlboro from Freehold or Lincroft should be able to explain which parts of its scope fall under permit requirements and which do not.
