Structural drying and dehumidification is the controlled removal of moisture from wood framing, drywall, subfloor and concrete after water has stopped flowing. Pumping out the puddle is only the first step. The water that soaked into the building materials stays behind until air movement and dehumidification pull it out.

Crews start with water extraction when water is still standing, then place equipment according to moisture readings, not guesswork. This page explains the equipment, the readings and how long drying usually takes.

How to dry out a house after a leak, and how long it takes

The honest answer to how long it takes to dry out a house after a leak is that it depends on what got wet. A small clean-water leak confined to one wall can dry in about three to five days. A floor-to-ceiling soaking, a wet subfloor or a concrete slab can run a week or more, and dense materials like hardwood and plaster dry slowest.

People also ask how long to run a dehumidifier after water damage. The answer is until the readings say stop, not until a calendar date. Walls that feel dry to the hand can still read high on a meter, and a house that is shut up too early can bring musty smells back.

Three things set the pace: how long the materials were wet, how humid the air is, and how much equipment can work in the affected rooms at once.

Water mitigation: the first stage of the drying job

Water mitigation means stopping the damage from growing. The source is shut off, standing water is extracted, wet contents are lifted, and affected materials are sorted into dry in place, open up or remove. Those decisions happen in the first visit and shape everything after.

A wet wall might be dried with the baseboard pulled and small holes drilled for airflow. A swollen section of wall and drywall that has lost its shape comes out instead. Insulation soaked with sewage or groundwater never gets a second chance. Mitigation decisions follow ANSI/IICRC S500, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to it.

Moisture mapping: measuring what you cannot see

A moisture map is a record of readings taken across walls, floors and ceilings. Pin and pinless meters read the wood and drywall, a thermal camera shows cold wet patches behind paint, and a hygrometer records air temperature and humidity.

Day one readings become the baseline. A reading on the same spot the next morning shows whether the material is drying or holding, so equipment can be added, moved or pulled. At the end, readings are compared to a dry standard for the same material in an unaffected room of your house. That comparison is what ends the job.

What each piece of drying equipment does

Each machine does one job, and the number placed depends on room size and wetness. The table shows how the main pieces work together.

Drying equipment and its job
EquipmentWhat it doesWhere it goes
Air moversPush a fast, low layer of air across wet surfaces so water leaves the materialAimed along walls and across floors, spaced by room size
LGR dehumidifierPulls water out of the air at low humidity, so drying keeps working after the surface is dryOne or more in a closed drying zone
Desiccant dehumidifierAbsorbs moisture chemically, useful when the air is cold or the job is stubbornUsed when the air is too cool for refrigerant machines
Drying mats and panelsPull water up through hardwood or from behind wallsFloors, cabinet toes, wall cavities
Thermal camera and metersFind wet zones and confirm when they are dryUsed each visit

An LGR (low grain refrigerant) machine takes the air to a much lower humidity than a household dehumidifier, which is why it dries framing that a basement unit never will.

What to do before renting drying equipment

We do not rent air movers or dehumidifiers, so we cannot send you equipment to run yourself. If you are thinking about renting, here is what the machines will and will not do.

A rented dehumidifier will lower humidity in a room. It will not tell you whether the joists behind the wall have reached a dry reading, where to aim airflow, or when it is safe to close up. A managed job adds those readings, plus the judgment to open a wall before mold has a chance. If you do rent, take your own meter readings daily.

The drying visit, day by day

Most structural drying jobs follow the same rhythm.

1

Day one: map and set

Water is stopped or extracted, wet areas are metered, and air movers and dehumidifiers are placed to match the map.

2

Day two: adjust

Fresh readings show what is responding. Machines move to the slow spots, and open walls or lifted baseboards get more airflow.

3

Day three and after: read and trim

Equipment is removed from areas that reached the dry standard while the stubborn spots keep running.

4

Final visit: confirm and release

Last readings are logged against the dry standard, equipment is collected, and you get the numbers before repairs start.

Where drying meets floors, walls and what comes next

Flooring is often the slowest part of a dry-out. Solid wood swells and cups as it absorbs water, so a damp board needs targeted drying. Our hardwood floor water damage page covers what can be saved and what cannot.

When readings reach the dry standard, the rebuild can begin. Drywall, trim and flooring installed over wet framing trap moisture and invite trouble later. That is why we hand you the logged numbers before anyone closes up a wall.

What drives the cost of structural drying

Cost follows the work, so scope and price are explained before work starts. Five things move it:

  • Water category: category 1 clean water, category 2 gray water and category 3 contaminated water carry different protocols.
  • Affected square footage and ceiling height, since larger volumes need more air movement and dehumidification.
  • Materials involved: drywall dries faster than hardwood, plaster or a concrete slab.
  • Drying days and equipment count, driven by daily readings.
  • Rebuild scope after drying, including drywall, flooring and paint.

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered by homeowners insurance, gradual leaks and neglect often are not, and flood damage is usually excluded and needs separate flood coverage. Check your policy and ask your insurer. Call (732) 352-9660 for a real number.

Drying in a Marlboro climate

NOAA's Freehold-Marlboro station records 48.57 inches of precipitation a year (1991 to 2020 normals), so outdoor air is often humid when a dry-out is under way. Closed doors and windows matter, and a dehumidifier in a sealed zone removes moisture more efficiently than an open house with fans running. In the 106 days a year at or below 32F, drying a frozen-pipe loss also means keeping rooms warm enough to dry, since cold air holds little moisture.
