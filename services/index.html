Start with the source: pipes and supply lines

When water comes from inside the plumbing, the first question is whether it is still running. A burst pipe can release gallons a minute until the main valve is closed. A frozen pipe may look harmless until it thaws and the split opens. Marlboro averages 106 days a year at or below 32F, so winter pipe failures are a real mechanism here.

If the water seems to come from under the floor, with warm spots or a meter that keeps spinning, read about a slab leak. If it comes from a tank, the water heater leak page covers the valve and drain pan steps.

Appliances, fixtures and the rooms around them

Appliance failures are usually smaller in volume but slower to notice. Washer and dishwasher hoses, refrigerator ice-maker lines and air conditioner drain lines leak into walls and floors for days. The appliance leak page covers laundry rooms and dishwashers, and the AC leak page covers condensate lines and attic air handlers.

Water under a sink or inside lower cabinets has its own page: kitchen water damage restoration deals with cabinet boxes, toe kicks and the floor beneath them.

Weather and water coming from outside

Outdoor water splits into two kinds. Wind and rain that get through a roof, window or wall are a storm water damage problem. Water that rises from the ground or a stream is flood damage restoration, which carries different contamination and insurance questions. The township reports about 4.9% of its land in the 1% annual-chance floodplain, so check the FEMA flood map for your address.

A wet basement can fit either one, so the basement water damage cleanup and crawl space pages cover the rooms themselves.

When the job is removing water or drying the structure

If your main problem is standing water on the floor, water extraction is the page about pumps and extraction units. If the surface looks dry but walls and subfloors are still wet, the structural drying and dehumidification page explains moisture mapping, air movers and LGR dehumidifiers.

Most real jobs use both: extract first, then dry. A sewage or contaminated backup is handled differently, so check sewage backup cleanup before touching anything.

Floors, walls and ceilings

Once the water is gone, the question is what to keep. Wood can cup, carpet pad holds moisture, and drywall wicks water upward. Our pages on hardwood floor repair, carpet restoration, wall and drywall repair and ceiling water damage each explain when drying works and when replacement makes more sense.

A sagging ceiling is urgent, since saturated drywall can come down, so call before touching it.

Businesses, rentals and shared buildings

A leak in a shop, office or multi-unit building has costs beyond the repair: lost days, tenant calls and neighbors affected. The commercial water damage restoration page covers planning around business hours and documenting a loss for a landlord or insurer.

If you manage a property, say so on the phone so the crew can plan access and which units are affected.

Mold after moisture

Mold needs moisture, so it usually shows up after a leak that was not fully dried. Mold remediation covers containment, removal and the published ANSI/IICRC S520 standard. Questions about health belong with a physician.

New Jersey's Department of Health says there are no State certification programs for mold companies, so ask any company how its crews work. Our about page lists the other standards we point homeowners to.
