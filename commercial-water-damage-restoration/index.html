Commercial water damage restoration is the extraction, drying and repair of a business property after a leak, flood or drain failure, done on a schedule that limits the days you are closed. If water is spreading right now, shut off the main valve, power off wet areas only if the panel is dry and safe, lift inventory and records off the floor, and keep staff and customers out. Dispatch is open 24/7.

Crews begin with water extraction and move straight to measured drying, so your space is usable as soon as the readings allow.

Water in the building right now: shut-off and protect

In a commercial building, the first call is often to the facilities contact, but the first action is the same as in a house: stop the water. Find the main shut-off for the unit, or the riser valve for the floor, and close it. In a multi-tenant building, tell the property manager at once, since the leak may start in another suite or on the roof.

Then protect what can be protected. Lift boxes, stock, files and computers off the floor, move them to dry ground, and unplug equipment standing in water only if the outlet is dry. Take photos of the wet areas and the damaged stock before anything is moved or thrown out.

Electrical and ceiling hazards in a wet commercial space

Commercial spaces carry more hidden electrical load than a house, and a wet ceiling tile can drop without warning. Treat the room as unsafe until a crew has looked.

  • Do not enter a room with standing water if a panel, floor outlet, server rack or large appliance is near the waterline.
  • Cut power at the panel only if it is dry and you can reach it without crossing water. Otherwise call 911 or the electric utility before you call us.
  • If you smell gas, evacuate the building and call 911 or the gas utility first.
  • Stay out from under bulging or stained ceiling tiles, which can fall when saturated.
  • Keep staff and customers out of the affected zone, and post a sign at the door.

What to do while you wait for the crew

We offer same-day response when available, so call as soon as you can describe what you see. Until help arrives:

1

Stop the source

Close the unit shut-off or riser valve, and call your plumber for a failed fixture. A restoration crew dries, it does not fix the pipe.

2

Isolate the damage

Close doors to wet rooms, turn off the HVAC fan if it is spreading damp air, and stop foot traffic.

3

Save the essentials

Move files, stock, laptops and anything that cannot be replaced off the floor and out of the room.

4

Photograph and list

Photograph the source, the water line and the damaged stock, and note what was in each area.

5

Call and describe

Call (732) 352-9660 and say how many rooms or suites are wet, whether neighbors are affected, and when you need the space open.

Small shop, office suite or multi-unit building: what changes

Scale changes the job. A single retail unit may need one crew and a day of extraction, while an office floor with open ceilings or a multi-unit building with wet shared walls needs staged work. A shop in a strip along Route 9 or Route 79 may share walls, a roof and sometimes a drain line with its neighbors, so a leak next door can become your leak.

In a multi-unit building, the crew maps which units are wet, in what order, and who is affected. Drying one unit while an adjacent wet wall stays wet only moves the water. The table shows how priorities shift by property type.

After-hours work and keeping downtime short

Every day a business is closed costs rent, payroll and customers. After-hours work, such as extraction at night or drying equipment placed when the building is empty, can shorten the closure. We can set the schedule so the sales floor or the front office reopens first while the back rooms keep drying.

Staging also matters for noise and access. Air movers and dehumidifiers hum all day, so we place them to keep walkways, exits and fire doors clear. Ask the crew for a plan that opens the highest-value areas first and tells you which rooms stay off limits.

Drying a commercial structure

Commercial floors and walls dry differently from a house. Carpet tile can often be lifted and dried or replaced by the tile, ceiling grid can be pulled and replaced, and concrete slabs release water slowly. Moisture meters, thermal imaging and LGR dehumidifiers guide the work, and daily readings decide when it ends.

Our structural drying process follows ANSI/IICRC S500, and you can ask any company whether its crews work to it. Larger buildings may need more machines and more days, and the plan states both before work starts.

Documentation that supports your claim and your lease

Records matter after the water is gone. We provide dated photos, moisture readings, a written scope of work and an equipment log, all in a form you can hand to your insurer, landlord or property manager.

We cannot promise how an insurer will respond, and we do not decide coverage. Check your policy for business property and business interruption wording, and ask your insurer what it needs. If you rent, read the lease to see who is responsible for the building and who for the contents.

Flooding versus a building leak

A burst line is a building problem. Rising water from outside is a different one. When rain and runoff push into a ground-floor space or a storage level, the water is usually Category 2 or 3 and needs the protocols described on our flood damage restoration page.

That also changes the insurance conversation. Commercial property policies generally treat flood separately, so check your policy and ask your insurer whether flood coverage is included. Document the water line and the source before cleanup starts.

What drives the cost of commercial water damage work

Every property is different, so scope and price are explained before work starts. These factors move the number:

  • Water category: clean supply water, gray water and sewage-contaminated water carry different protocols.
  • Affected area and building type: one suite, a full floor or several units.
  • Materials involved: carpet tile, ceiling grid, drywall, flooring, stock and equipment.
  • Drying days and equipment: more volume and thick materials mean more machines and more days.
  • After-hours scheduling and rebuild scope, including repairs and repainting.
